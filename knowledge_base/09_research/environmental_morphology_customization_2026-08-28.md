# Environmental Control for Mushroom Morphology and Quality

## Scope

Research intake on deliberate manipulation of fruiting environment — relative humidity (RH), CO2/ventilation, light intensity/photoperiod/spectrum and temperature — to steer morphology, development, yield and quality in legal edible/medicinal cultivated mushrooms. The operational concept is **phenotype steering**: environmental variables are experimental factors, not universal species setpoints.

This document does not authorize production setpoint changes. Values from literature remain bounded by species, strain, substrate, block geometry, developmental stage, chamber design and measurement method until locally validated.

## Core synthesis

### 1. CO2 is a high-leverage morphology variable

CO2 concentration is best treated as a proxy/control variable for gas exchange during fruiting. In many cultivated basidiomycetes, elevated CO2 during fruit-body development can shift allocation toward stem/elongated tissue and away from normal cap expansion or branching. Response is strongly species- and stage-dependent. Therefore a single CO2 threshold cannot define quality across Pleurotus, Hericium and Lentinula.

Operational implication: capture continuous CO2 and explicitly associate exposure windows with morphology. Avoid treating FAE rate alone as the biological variable because chamber volume, occupancy, respiration, leakage and fan duty cycle alter the resulting CO2 concentration.

### 2. RH controls surface water balance and interacts with airflow

RH affects evaporation from developing primordia and fruit bodies. High RH is generally needed around initiation and early development, but persistent near-saturation plus weak air movement can increase surface wetness and quality/contamination problems. Lower RH and/or excessive local air velocity can cause drying, cracking, aborted primordia or reduced tissue quality.

Operational implication: RH must be interpreted with temperature, local air movement, humidifier cycling and condensation observations. Record visible condensation and surface condition rather than relying on RH alone.

### 3. Temperature is both a developmental signal and a growth-rate constraint

Temperature can alter initiation, development rate, morphology, yield distribution and quality. Species and strains differ materially. Shiitake induction is especially process- and strain-dependent; Hericium morphology and Pleurotus development likewise cannot be reduced to one universal fruiting temperature.

Operational implication: distinguish induction temperature from subsequent fruit-body development temperature and record actual time-temperature exposure rather than only nominal setpoints.

### 4. Light is a morphogenetic signal, not primarily an energy input

Cultivated mushrooms use light as developmental information. Relevant descriptors include illuminance (lux), photoperiod and, where evidence supports it, wavelength/spectrum. Light requirements and responses vary by species and stage. Lux values from one lamp/spectrum are not fully interchangeable with another spectrum.

Operational implication: Setas OS experiments should record lamp/source, photoperiod and measured illuminance at crop level; spectrum/CCT should be recorded when known. Do not infer a universal lux optimum.

### 5. Environmental variables interact

The phenotype observed at harvest is generated by interacting variables rather than independent sliders. CO2 response depends on developmental stage and airflow; RH response depends on temperature and air velocity; temperature changes respiration and therefore chamber CO2 load; light effects can depend on developmental stage and exposure duration.

The appropriate experimental object is therefore a **time-resolved environmental recipe** rather than four static setpoints.

## Species-specific working hypotheses

### Hericium erinaceus

Priority phenotype dimensions: compactness versus elongation/branching, spine initiation and length, density, color, surface drying, fresh mass and marketable fraction.

Existing KB evidence already bounds one published fruiting regime at CO2 <1200 ppm, 18 ± 2 °C, 85–90% RH and 1000 lux/8 h. That regime is a reference condition, not proof that 1200 ppm is a causal morphology threshold.

Working hypothesis: CO2 exposure during specific developmental windows is likely one of the most useful controllable drivers for Hericium form, but strain-specific local trials are required before encoding morphology targets.

### Pleurotus spp.

Priority phenotype dimensions: stipe length/thickness, cap diameter, cap-to-stipe ratio, cluster density, cap shape, color, dry edges and marketable yield.

Working hypothesis: CO2/FAE and light are strong candidate morphology levers, with temperature and RH defining the feasible envelope and tissue quality. Species/strain identity must remain explicit because P. ostreatus and warm-fruiting Pleurotus can respond differently.

### Lentinula edodes

Priority phenotype dimensions: cap diameter/thickness, stem length, cap cracking/pattern, firmness, color, flush timing, yield and hydration response.

Working hypothesis: temperature and hydration/induction history are especially important; RH and ventilation then shape fruit-body quality. Existing KB evidence shows induction response is inseparable from block mass, geometry, maturity and hydration dynamics.

## Proposed experimental architecture

Use sequential experiments rather than immediately running a full four-factor factorial.

**Phase A — baseline characterization.** Hold the current species/strain/substrate/process constant. Log T, RH, CO2 and light at crop level throughout fruiting and photograph fruit bodies at fixed intervals. Establish natural chamber variability and phenotype variance.

**Phase B — single-variable sensitivity screens.** Change one environmental factor across conservative literature-bounded levels while maintaining the others near baseline. The objective is to estimate direction and magnitude of phenotype response and identify unsafe/unproductive regions.

**Phase C — interaction trials.** Test the strongest two factors using a small factorial or response-surface design. Likely candidates are CO2 × RH for Hericium/Pleurotus and temperature × hydration/induction for shiitake.

**Phase D — time-window steering.** Once causal responses are reproducible, vary environmental conditions by developmental stage: initiation, early expansion and maturation. This is the step that can produce deliberate phenotype profiles rather than generic chamber optimization.

## Minimum experiment schema for Setas OS

Each experimental unit should preserve:

- species and strain identity;
- spawn batch and substrate/formulation;
- bag/filter, geometry and wet block mass;
- inoculation date, full-colonization/maturity state and fruiting/induction date;
- chamber and position;
- temperature time series;
- RH time series;
- CO2 time series;
- light source, photoperiod and crop-level lux; spectrum/CCT where available;
- fan/exhaust and humidifier duty or event log;
- intervention timestamps;
- contamination/abnormality events;
- standardized photographs with scale;
- morphology measurements appropriate to species;
- harvest mass, marketable mass, BE where meaningful, and flush number;
- operator sensory/quality observations as explicitly subjective fields.

## Morphology metrics

A useful phenotype system must move beyond yield alone. Suggested machine-readable metrics:

**Hericium:** fruit-body bounding dimensions, compactness index, branch count/density, spine length distribution, whiteness/color score, surface-dryness defect, fresh mass.

**Pleurotus:** cap diameter, stipe length and diameter, cap:stipe ratio, cluster count, fruit count, cap curvature, color, fresh mass and marketable fraction.

**Shiitake:** cap diameter and thickness, stipe length, cap:stipe mass ratio where feasible, cracking/pattern score, firmness proxy, fresh mass, water uptake during induction and flush timing.

Standardized imaging can later support computer-vision phenotype extraction, but manual measurements should remain the validation reference during pilot development.

## Control-system implications

Environmental control should support target bands, hysteresis and stage-specific profiles rather than rapid switching around single-point targets. Sensor placement matters: crop-level conditions can differ from room-average conditions, especially near humidifier outlets, returns, doors and densely loaded racks.

For the existing Setas de la Peña sensor stack, CO2, RH and temperature logging are already conceptually compatible with this research direction. Light measurement should be added as a calibrated experimental observation if morphology steering by illumination is pursued.

Do not encode literature values directly as autonomous control rules. The safe path is evidence -> local trial -> replicated result -> operational promotion.

## Evidence grading and boundaries

**Higher confidence:** environmental conditions materially affect fruiting development and morphology; CO2/ventilation, RH, temperature and light are legitimate experimental variables; responses depend on species/strain and developmental stage.

**Moderate confidence:** targeted environmental profiles can reproducibly bias specific market-relevant morphological traits when genotype, substrate and process are controlled.

**Needs local validation:** exact CO2 thresholds for desired Hericium form; exact lux/spectrum targets; optimal RH trajectories; transferable temperature recipes; interaction coefficients between variables; phenotype targets that remain stable across substrate batches and chamber loading.

## Research-to-operations gate

No environmental treatment becomes a canonical Setas de la Peña setpoint until it has: (1) source-bounded rationale, (2) locally recorded treatment conditions, (3) replicated biological outcome, (4) contamination and quality assessment, and (5) explicit promotion through the KB approval path.

## Immediate KB candidates

The strongest transferable findings should enter the Active Research Knowledge registry as advisory entries: time-resolved environmental recipes; CO2 as a morphology factor rather than a universal threshold; RH interpreted with condensation/air movement; light recorded as source + photoperiod + crop-level illuminance; and standardized morphology measurement as required experimental metadata.
